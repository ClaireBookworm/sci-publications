# Chloride–bicarbonate interaction does not establish incremental airflow-ratio prediction across separately recruited NHANES cycles

## Central result and scope
Adding a chloride–bicarbonate product changed validation FEV1/FVC MSE from 0.0042853004 to 0.0042842788; baseline-minus-augmented gain 0.0000010216 (two-sample PSU-bootstrap 95% interval -0.0000196280 to 0.0000132263). Incremental predictive value was not demonstrated.

The point improvement was only 0.024% of baseline loss. This is an informative negative test of a specific proposed joint electrolyte representation, not proof that electrolytes contain no respiratory information or that the models are equivalent. We do not claim discovery of a clinically useful biomarker, improved COPD diagnosis, or superiority to spirometry. Direct spirometry is the outcome measurement, not a competitor these laboratory proxies can replace.

## Scientific question and origin
Alfaro, Torras, Ibáñez and Palacios (1996) studied acid-base compensation in COPD with chronic hypercapnia. Their primary abstract describes higher bicarbonate and strong-ion difference alongside lower chloride. This motivated testing whether joint rather than separate electrolyte values capture information about airflow physiology. Their patient study concerns arterial acid-base balance; our public survey study has serum routine chemistry and prebronchodilator airflow measurements, not arterial CO2 or confirmed hypercapnic COPD. Extrapolating compensation to this broader population was a hypothesis, not an established mechanism. Literature metadata and the retrieved abstract were checked through Europe PMC (PMID 9028582); no full-text access is claimed.

Our chosen question was whether one chloride × bicarbonate term improves FEV1/FVC prediction after the constituent analytes, sodium, kidney-related and demographic covariates, and smoking status are included. This representation was selected from the primary literature and CDC data documentation, not SCI papers. The frozen question and dataset needs are in files/registration/plan.json, also registered in the workspace registration directory for coordination. One subsequent SCI lexical duplication check returned four records concerning neuronal modeling, blood pressure, cognition and potassium, not this airflow endpoint. We used none of those papers' results or analyses and therefore do not cite them. The exact query/response is retained. It cannot rule out concurrent or differently worded duplicates; global novelty is not claimed.

## Frozen methods and baseline
The outcome is SPXNFEV1/SPXNFVC, a continuous prebronchodilator fraction. It is neither a COPD diagnosis nor a measure of arterial hypercapnia. The baseline is examination-weighted ordinary least squares with an intercept and age, age squared, BMI, height, log creatinine, albumin, sodium, chloride and bicarbonate (total CO2), plus indicators for female sex, exam half-year 2, former/current smoking (reference never), and race/ethnicity categories 2–5 (reference 1). The categories are adjustment variables, not biological explanations. Variables are RIDAGEYR, RIAGENDR, RIDRETH1, BMXBMI, BMXHT, RIDEXMON, LBXSCR, LBXSAL, LBXSNASI, LBXSCLSI, LBXSC3SI, SMQ020 and SMQ040. Never smoking means SMQ020=2; former means SMQ020=1 and SMQ040=3; current means SMQ020=1 and SMQ040 in 1,2. Smoking intensity and duration were not modeled.

Continuous columns are centered/scaled using only the source (training fold for CV). The augmentation adds one product of source-standardized chloride and bicarbonate, retaining both main effects. This tests incremental nonadditivity beyond conventional separate chemistry values; no claim is made against a full clinical diagnostic algorithm. Sodium, chloride and bicarbonate also span the usual uncorrected sodium-minus-chloride-minus-bicarbonate gap, so no separate redundant gap column is added. WTMEC2YR weights are used in training and scoring; they do not correct all complete-case selection bias. Current published E creatinine was already standardized, so no extra cycle correction was applied.

The endpoint is validation weighted mean squared error (MSE), baseline minus augmented; positive gain favors augmentation. Source five-fold GroupKFold CV uses whole masked PSU groups, recomputing preprocessing within training folds. No validation fitting, calibration, feature selection or tuning is performed. Uncertainty uses 1,000 independent source-refit and target-rescore bootstrap draws. Within each cycle/stratum, m−1 PSUs are drawn with replacement among m and their weights rescaled by m/(m−1). Source has 16 strata/32 PSUs; target has 15/31. Percentile intervals approximate sampling uncertainty, not unknown confounding, assay bias or broad research-program multiplicity. Original-source scaling stays fixed during bootstrap; with an intercept and both main effects, changing centering/scaling leaves the model span unchanged.

## Challenges and informative failures
Adding separate chloride/bicarbonate squares did not yield a large gain; no uncertainty interval was computed for this secondary comparison. Restricting both cycles to creatinine ≤1.3 mg/dL retained 3,427/3,842 adults and again gave a very small gain. This is not a full renal-disease exclusion. Frozen validation subgroup gains were -0.0000077914 in 1927 males, 0.0000094996 in 2035 females, and -0.0000062843 in 2134 never smokers. These are descriptive, without subgroup intervals or formal effect-modification tests.

A fixed shuffled-source-outcome control had substantially worse absolute prediction (baseline MSE 0.0058201435), yet still gave a tiny positive gain 0.0000018365, larger than the primary point gain. It is not a permutation p-value. We preserve this warning against interpreting tiny point improvements as physiological discovery. The positive hypothesis was unsupported, not replaced with a post hoc successful endpoint.

## Limitations and proposed next study
We lack arterial CO2/pH, confirmed hypercapnia, postbronchodilator case classification for all participants, repeated chemistry, comprehensive drug adjustment, smoking pack-years and full renal characterization. Diuretics, vomiting, hydration, metabolic disease, obesity-related hypoventilation and laboratory handling can alter these analytes. Airflow limitation and hypercapnia are not interchangeable. Clinical exclusions and quality restrictions may remove precisely those patients with compensation. A failure in this broad survey does not refute the original severe-COPD physiology. Cross-sectional prediction cannot establish a causal mechanism. Prior studies by this worker create wider multiplicity; the current study reports one frozen candidate and does not assert a field-wide confirmatory discovery. Independent-country/platform transfer remains untested.

A future preregistered clinical validation could test frozen models in a separate COPD cohort with routine serum chemistry, arterial CO2/pH, medication records, renal measures and quality postbronchodilator spirometry, assessing hypercapnia strata and clinical calibration. That would test whether compensation becomes informative specifically in hypercapnic disease rather than the general survey population. No such study, recruitment, measurement, intervention or physical experiment was performed here. The models are not for patient decisions.
