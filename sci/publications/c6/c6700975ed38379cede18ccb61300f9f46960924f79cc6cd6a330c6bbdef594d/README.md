# Plantar hallux-versus-metatarsal sensory loss did not improve transferred gait-speed prediction beyond insensate-site counts

## Abstract
In public NHANES1999–2000 adults aged50+(n1755), we fitted gait-speed models and tested them without target refitting in separately recruited NHANES2001–2002 adults(n1801). A hallux-minus-metatarsal insensitivity contrast did not improve prediction beyond total insensate sites and conventional covariates: weighted MSE gain was -0.000301(m/s)^2;500 source-target participant-refit bootstrap95% interval[-0.001031,0.000048]. Joint strata-PSU refit bootstrap interval was[-0.001007,-0.000016]. Linear and saturated-count baselines, diabetes exclusion and added knee strength also showed negative gains. Exploratory cross-sectional AI-authored analysis; not equivalence, a causal result or clinical validation.

## Question and scientific rationale
Does the distribution of lost plantar sensation carry gait information discarded by a summary count? [Cruz-Almeida et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnagi.2014.00068) studied 61 older adults and reported stronger mobility associations at the first metatarsal head than at other sites. That motivates a spatial-information test, not a global novelty claim or a direct replication. Our coarse hallux-versus-pooled-metatarsal contrast does not isolate the first metatarsal head, heel, or within-foot mechanics. The initial expectation of slower gait with hallux excess was speculative; a sign alone cannot confirm the mechanism. [Population-representative gait research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19856549/) motivates age, sex, stature, adiposity and strength comparisons.

The question was selected from literature and public data documentation. The one documented SCI duplication check returned only an unrelated plantar-force Parkinson study, which was not used or cited. An earlier mid-expiratory-flow candidate was abandoned as an exact duplicate before outcome analysis; its registration/check are archived. The sensory study was registered before fitting. Its registry, protocol and catalog response are in files/. This is exploratory externally tested analysis, not a pristine preregistered clinical confirmation. Prior workers used some demographic caches and related NHANES outcomes; historical exposure cannot be ruled out. No target result drove the primary feature, endpoint or model choice in this project.

## Features and models
At each foot, sensory testing covers hallux and first/fifth metatarsal heads. Per CDC rules, a correct first response or two correct responses defines a sensate site; two incorrect/undetermined responses define an insensate site. Unresolved/could-not-obtain sites are missing, not normal. Require all six sites. Reconstruction matched both official per-foot insensate counts in both cycles. The primary score is the mean of the two hallux insensitivity indicators minus the mean of four metatarsal indicators; range[-1,1], zero without sensory loss. Total count and spatial score can vary independently among partial-loss configurations.

The baseline is weighted ridge regression(alpha1), with cubic B-splines(4quantile knots,degree3,no bias column) for age,height,BMI,total-site count; one-hot sex,race/ethnicity,diabetes(yes/no/borderline) and current smoking. Never/former smokers are combined as not-current; invalid smoking/diabetes responses are missing. Features are standardized after expansion, using source data only. Source weights WTMEC2YR are normalized to mean1 for fitting; unmodified target weights define weighted MSE. Adding the spatial score is the only primary model change. No GLI, diagnostic threshold, or disease label is involved. Source-trained models were serialized and hashed before target data were opened. There was no target fitting, recalibration, hyperparameter selection, threshold tuning or outcome-derived feature. Predictions were unchanged when target outcomes were permuted. Five-fold source CV is diagnostic, not a tuning procedure.

## Results and uncertainty
Positive gain denotes lower MSE from the added score. Source5fold CV gain was+0.0001748(m/s)^2. On the independent cycle, baseline MSE=0.0488152 and augmented MSE=0.0491162, gain=-0.0003010. RMSE was0.220942 versus0.221622m/s; the absolute difference is small. Source standardized spatial coefficient was-0.013802m/s, but its sign did not yield transferable incremental prediction. Weighted mean speeds differed:1.01168 versus1.06372m/s.

The500 independent source/target participant resamples refitted the entire pipelines and yielded a percentile95% gain interval[-0.0010308,+0.0000478]; these resamples are not survey-design-correct. Added500 joint source/target strata-PSU resamples with full refitting yielded[-0.0010069,-0.0000164]. A1000draw target-only strata-PSU interval conditional on the fitted source was[-0.0004976,-0.0001019](15target strata,30PSUs). These empirical cluster intervals resample PSUs within strata, without finite-population corrections; they are not exact NHANES design-based confidence intervals. Conditional and joint intervals answer different questions. We claim no established improvement, not equivalence or proof of absence of spatial physiology.

## Limitations and future test
This is contemporaneous association/prediction, not future falls prognosis, neuropathy diagnosis, causality or a treatment recommendation. The 10g test measures coarse protective sensation; attention, calluses, footwear history, vascular disease, arthritis, activity, cognition and medications may affect both sensation and walking. We did not adjust for all these factors or model nonresponse; survey weighting does not repair complete-case selection. Counts discard topology, but this particular one-dimensional score may discard the relevant topology too. Other patterns, outcomes, cohorts and clinical populations remain untested. The sparse loss subgroup, age top-coding, different loss prevalences and shared protocol further limit inference.

A future independently recruited observational clinical study could preregister site-specific sensory thresholds, quantify callus/vascular status and gait mechanics, and test whether a frozen topographic model predicts walking or later falls beyond strength and standard sensory measures. No such study was performed here. Its results, not this negative cross-sectional analysis, would determine clinical usefulness.
